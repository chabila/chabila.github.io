Using AI in business analysis for UAT and testing

Testing is the one phase where "AI drafted it" and "AI verified it" are completely different claims. This is a walk through every stage — prep, execution, UAT, release — with an honest line on which side of that gap each task sits.

Chabila Borkar· Senior Business Analyst, Payments & eCommerce
A quick note before you dive in

I've sat in enough UAT sessions where a stakeholder asks "did we test the case where the refund fails after the customer's already been emailed?" and the honest answer was no, not yet, good catch. That question came from someone who understood the business, not from a scenario generator. AI is genuinely useful across most of what follows — it's just not a substitute for that instinct.

This is organised exactly how the work actually happens: preparation, execution, UAT, release. No reordering for the sake of a tidier framework.

Part 1 — Fundamentals

Test preparation

This is where AI's leverage is highest — turning specs and tickets into structured test artefacts is exactly the kind of transformation task it's built for. The risk is trusting the output's completeness rather than its speed.

✓Requirements
AI drafts a plain-English summary of a spec or ticket thread fast. It's a comprehension check, not a substitute for reading the actual requirement yourself before you sign off on scope.
✓Acceptance criteria
Turns loose requirements into Given/When/Then drafts in seconds. Check each one is actually testable — AI writes grammatically correct criteria that are sometimes untestable as written.
✓Happy paths
Reliable territory — the standard successful flow is usually well represented in whatever spec you feed it. Quick to generate, quick to verify.
✓Negative scenarios
Good first pass on generic failure modes (invalid input, declined payment, timeout). The negative scenarios that came from your actual past incidents won't show up unless you feed that history in.
✓Edge cases
Strong on textbook boundaries — min/max, null, empty, concurrency. Weak on the edge cases that only exist because of a business rule nobody wrote down. That gap is yours to fill.
✓Integration scenarios
Can map candidate touchpoints from architecture docs into scenarios, but it's guessing at sequence unless you give it the actual sequence diagram or API contract.
✓Regression scenarios
Genuinely useful for shortlisting prior defect-prone areas from your defect history — this is a data task AI is well suited to, if the historical data is actually available to it.
✓Data validation
Drafts field-level checks from a data dictionary reliably. Production data has quirks no dictionary documents — that part still needs someone who's looked at the real data.
Every item above follows the same shape: AI compresses the first draft; you still own the judgement about whether it's complete. That pattern holds for the rest of this guide too.

Execution

Less drafting, more pattern-matching against volume — this is where AI's speed at reading through noise (logs, payloads, screenshots) starts to matter more than its writing.

✓Execute scenarios
AI doesn't click through your app for you unless you've wired in an agentic testing tool. What it can do is pre-fill test-step scripts and data, so execution is mechanical rather than something you're improvising.
✓Compare expected vs actual
One of the strongest use cases in this whole guide — diffing large payloads or screens and flagging deviations a tired human eye would skim straight past.
✓Capture evidence
Summarising screenshots and logs into a readable evidence note attached to the test case — saves the "let me write this up properly later" step that usually never happens.
✓Reproduce defects
Can suggest a minimal repro path from a messy bug report by cross-referencing the steps described. Still needs a human to actually confirm it reproduces before it goes anywhere near a defect ticket.
✓Inspect logs/data
The single biggest time-saver on this list — searching thousands of log lines for an anomaly pattern in seconds instead of a grep session that eats your afternoon.
✓Raise Jira defect
Drafts a structured defect — steps, expected/actual, a suggested severity — from your raw notes. Severity and priority still need someone who knows the business impact, which AI doesn't.

UAT

This is the phase with the least AI leverage, and that's the honest takeaway, not a caveat. UAT exists because business judgement can't be automated — the value here is almost entirely human.

✓Identify business scenarios
AI can surface candidate scenarios from usage analytics or support ticket patterns. Which ones actually matter enough for business sign-off is a judgement call that stays with the BA.
✓Coordinate business users
Purely mechanical — drafting invites, session plans, calendar logistics. Low-risk delegation, hand it over freely.
✓Facilitate sessions
Not a delegation task. An AI note-taker can transcribe and summarise the session for you afterwards, but reading the room while a stakeholder gets frustrated isn't something to hand off.
✓Track defects
AI is genuinely helpful at consolidating scattered feedback from multiple users into one deduped, tracked list — the tedious admin part, not the judgement part.
✓Retest fixes
Can do a first-pass check that a fix's diff actually addresses the reported repro steps. Confirming it resolves the issue for the business scenario that mattered is still a human call.
✓Obtain sign-off
Entirely human, no exceptions. Sign-off is an accountability act, not a drafting task — there's no version of this AI should touch.

Release

Back to AI having real leverage — mostly because this phase is about triage under time pressure, and triage is a pattern-matching problem.

✓Regression
Risk-based test selection from the change diff and defect history — a genuinely useful alternative to "run the whole suite because we're not sure what changed."
✓Smoke testing
Drafting a lean smoke suite covering the highest-risk paths from the change log — quick to generate, quick to sanity-check.
✓Production validation
Comparing live output against expected baselines is a solid anomaly-detection use case right after a release window.
✓Post-release monitoring
Anomaly detection on live metrics and logs, surfacing what's actually worth attention instead of a human staring at a dashboard for two hours.
Part 2 — Enhancements

Once the basics are solid, these are worth the setup time

Three techniques that solve specific problems once you're doing this at scale across a real release cadence — not because they're fashionable, but because each closes a gap the checklist above runs into eventually.

Grounding

Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Point the model at your actual specs, past defect history, and prior UAT feedback logs, and have it answer from those instead of guessing generically. The difference between "AI invents a plausible edge case" and "AI surfaces the edge case that broke prod in March, because it read the incident report."

Maps to: Test preparation & regression — scenarios grounded in what actually broke before, not a generic checklist.
Control

Human-in-the-loop

Defect severity classification, UAT sign-off, and production release decisions are exactly the checkpoints where an unreviewed AI call is the wrong risk to take. HITL means the workflow pauses at those specific points and waits for a named human — not everywhere, just where it counts.

Maps to: UAT sign-off & release — the two places "mostly right" isn't an acceptable bar.
Connection

MCP servers

Model Context Protocol gives an AI assistant live, permissioned access to Jira, your test management tool, or your log platform directly — instead of exporting a CSV every time you want current defect or execution data. It queries the real system, scoped to your access.

Maps to: Execution & release — live defect and log data pulled on demand instead of stale exports.
Part 3 — Hands-on

Building this in Microsoft Foundry

The sequence I'd actually follow to wire the three pieces above into one assistant that supports the whole test lifecycle, rather than three disconnected demos. Microsoft Foundry (formerly Azure AI Foundry), current as of 2026.

Create a Foundry project and connect your test artefacts

Stand up a project, then connect your specs, defect history, and past UAT feedback logs — via Azure Blob Storage or a data connection — ready to be indexed.

Deploy a model from the catalog

Pick a model (e.g. gpt-4o) from the Foundry model catalog as your reasoning engine — swappable later without touching the rest of the setup.

Ground it against your own history (RAG)

Index specs, defect history, and incident write-ups with Azure AI Search, add it to your agent as a knowledge source, so generated scenarios cite the actual source rather than a generic pattern.

Attach live systems via MCP

From the Foundry portal's Tools menu, add an MCP server connection to Jira or your test management tool, so the agent can read current defect status directly rather than working from a stale export.

Add a human-approval checkpoint

Flag defect severity classification and any release-readiness output as requiring approval. The workflow pauses on a function_approval_request and only proceeds once a named reviewer signs off.

Orchestrate it as a workflow, not a one-shot prompt

Use Foundry's workflow builder to chain retrieval → scenario drafting → execution support → defect drafting → human review, as a repeatable process across every release, not a one-off script.

Evaluate before you trust it, then monitor

Run Foundry's built-in groundedness and relevance evaluations before rollout, then use tracing and Application Insights to see what it's actually doing once it's live.
