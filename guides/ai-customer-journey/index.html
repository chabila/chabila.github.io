Field notes from a payments BA

The end-to-end customer journey, stage by stage

Checkout gets most of a payments BA's attention because that's where the money changes hands — but a customer's journey starts long before that and doesn't end at confirmation. This is the full arc: seven stages, what a BA actually does at each, and where AI genuinely earns a place.

Chabila Borkar· Senior Business Analyst, Payments & eCommerce
A quick note before you dive in

The mistake I made early on was treating each stage as its own project with its own team and its own metrics — which is how you end up with a beautifully optimised checkout sitting downstream of a browse experience nobody's looked at in two years. The customer doesn't experience stages. They experience one continuous thing, and a drop at stage five sometimes traces back to a decision made at stage two.

Checkout and payment integration get a much deeper treatment in Guides 001 and 002 — here they're one stage among seven, in proportion to the rest of the journey.

Part 1 — Fundamentals

Awareness & acquisition

The journey starts before there's a "customer" at all — just a visitor arriving from somewhere, for some reason. A BA's job here is making sure that reason is captured accurately enough to mean something later.

WHAT'S HAPPENING
What I'm actually doing

Defining what counts as a qualified visit

Working with marketing on campaign attribution rules, UTM tagging standards, and what "engaged" means well enough that reporting downstream is trustworthy.

Where AI earns its keep

Turning a campaign brief into a first-draft tagging and tracking requirement spec — genuinely fast, and easy for a human to check against the brief.

Discovery & browse

This is where most of the journey's time actually gets spent, and where a surprising amount of downstream cart and checkout behaviour is quietly decided.

WHAT'S HAPPENING
What I'm actually doing

Specifying search, filter, and ranking logic

Writing requirements for product listing page ranking, filter/facet behaviour, and category taxonomy — the plumbing that decides what a customer actually sees.

Where AI earns its keep

Drafting search relevance test cases from real query logs, and summarising session-replay drop-off patterns on the PLP into candidate causes worth investigating.

Consideration & cart

The decision-making stage — cart abandonment gets studied more than almost anything else in eCommerce, for good reason.

WHAT'S HAPPENING
What I'm actually doing

Defining cart persistence, cross-sell, and wishlist rules

Specifying how a cart survives across sessions and devices, and the business logic behind cross-sell/upsell placement.

Where AI earns its keep

Clustering abandonment reasons from analytics and support tickets into root-cause candidates — the same pattern-matching-at-volume strength that shows up everywhere in this series.

Checkout & payment

Two full guides already cover this in depth, so it stays brief here — proportionate to its place as one stage in seven, not the whole journey.

The detailed checkout funnel breakdown lives in Guide 001, and the payment integration lifecycle — PSP discovery through go-live — in Guide 002. Both apply directly here.

Fulfilment & delivery

The promise made at checkout gets kept — or doesn't — here. This is where OMS logic and carrier integration quietly decide customer trust for the next purchase.

WHAT'S HAPPENING
What I'm actually doing

Mapping order state transitions and carrier logic

Documenting OMS order states, split-shipment handling, delivery promise date rules, and carrier selection logic across markets.

Where AI earns its keep

A first-pass integration mapping from a carrier's API docs — the same pattern as PSP mapping in Guide 002, just a different vendor. Summarising delivery exception logs into a pattern list ops can actually act on.

Post-purchase & support

Where a lot of customer sentiment actually gets formed — often more than at checkout itself, because this is where something's already gone slightly wrong.

WHAT'S HAPPENING
What I'm actually doing

Defining returns/refund rules and support taxonomy

Specifying return-reason business rules, refund eligibility logic, and the categorisation taxonomy support agents actually use day to day.

Where AI earns its keep

Drafting a return-reason taxonomy from raw free-text ticket data, and summarising long support threads into a structured case note — real time saved on genuinely tedious work.

Retention & loyalty

The stage that turns a transaction into a relationship — or fails to, which is usually more visible in the data than anyone expects.

WHAT'S HAPPENING
What I'm actually doing

Specifying loyalty tiers and re-engagement triggers

Defining loyalty tier logic, personalisation/segmentation rules, and the triggers behind win-back campaigns.

Where AI earns its keep

Drafting segment definitions from behavioural data and turning a marketing brief into a structured personalisation rule table — a first draft, always checked against actual customer data before it ships.

Seven stages, one customer. The single most useful thing a BA can do across all of them is trace a metric back through the stage before it, not just the stage it appeared in. AI's summarising strength makes that tracing faster — it doesn't replace the instinct to go looking.
Before you touch a model

What holds true across the whole journey

01

Journey data lives in more systems than you think

CRM, OMS, PSP, support tool, loyalty platform — stitching a single customer's path across all of them is genuinely the job. No model does that stitching for you unless you've connected the systems.

02

PII spans the whole journey, not just checkout

Marketing, support, and loyalty data all carry privacy obligations of their own — this is a bigger data-handling surface than payment data alone, not a smaller one.

03

A drop at one stage can be caused by an earlier one

Checkout abandonment sometimes traces back to a browse-stage search relevance problem. Investigate the stage before the symptom, not just the symptom.

04

The customer never sees your org chart

Seven stages usually means seven different teams. The BA's real value is often just being the one person who can see the whole thing at once.

Part 2 — Enhancements

Once the basics are solid, these are worth the setup time

The same three techniques from the rest of the series, applied at the scale a full customer journey actually needs.

Grounding

Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Ground the model in your own journey data — past campaign attribution reports, PLP analytics, support ticket history, loyalty programme rules — instead of general knowledge. The difference between a generic "customers abandon carts due to shipping cost" answer and one that cites your actual Q2 exit-survey data.

Maps to: every stage — grounded in the specific data that stage produces.
Control

Human-in-the-loop

Refund approval, loyalty tier overrides, and discount/goodwill decisions carry real commercial and reputational weight. HITL means those specific actions pause for a named human, while lower-stakes drafting keeps moving without a bottleneck.

Maps to: post-purchase support & loyalty — where individual customer-facing decisions live.
Connection

MCP servers

An agent with live, permissioned access to your CRM, OMS, support platform, and marketing tool can trace a customer's actual path across all seven stages on demand — instead of you exporting five different reports and joining them by hand.

Maps to: cross-stage investigation — tracing a metric back through the journey that produced it.
Part 3 — Hands-on

Building this in Microsoft Foundry

The sequence I'd follow to build one assistant that can see the whole customer journey, not just whichever system happens to be open. Microsoft Foundry (formerly Azure AI Foundry), current as of 2026.

Create a Foundry project and connect your journey data

Stand up a project, then connect campaign attribution reports, PLP analytics, order and delivery data, support tickets, and loyalty programme rules — via Azure Blob Storage or a data connection — ready to be indexed.

Deploy a model from the catalog

Pick a model (e.g. gpt-4o) from the Foundry model catalog as your reasoning engine — swappable later without rebuilding the rest.

Ground it against your own journey history (RAG)

Index the connected sources with Azure AI Search, add it to your agent as a knowledge source, so any answer about "why did stage X drop" is grounded in your actual cross-stage data.

Attach live systems via MCP

From the Foundry portal's Tools menu, connect your CRM, OMS, and support platform as tool connections, so the agent can pull a specific customer's live journey rather than working from a stale export.

Add human-approval checkpoints where the stakes are real

Flag refund approvals, loyalty overrides, and goodwill gestures as requiring approval. The workflow pauses on a function_approval_request and only proceeds once a named reviewer signs off.

Orchestrate it as a cross-stage workflow

Use Foundry's workflow builder to chain retrieval → cross-stage analysis → drafting → human review, so a single query can trace a customer's path across all seven stages in one pass.

Evaluate before you trust it, then monitor

Run Foundry's built-in groundedness and relevance evaluations before rollout, then use tracing and Application Insights to see what it's actually surfacing once it's live.

If you've mapped your own customer journey and found a stage that doesn't fit neatly into these seven — that's usually where the interesting work is. Happy to compare notes.
