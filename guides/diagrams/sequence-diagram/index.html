Diagrams · Guide 02

UML sequence diagrams, built one level at a time

A sequence diagram shows who talks to whom, in what order. We build one card payment flow in five levels, from a three-party exchange to 3DS authentication and asynchronous webhooks with retries. New elements are outlined in green at each level.

Chabila Borkar · Senior Business Analyst, Payments & eCommerce
Where this fits

The BPMN guide shows business process across roles. This one zooms into system interactions: the API calls and responses between systems. The flow is a simplified, generic card payment, so check the exact steps against your PSP's documentation.

The building blocks

Time runs from top to bottom. Everything else is a participant, a message or a frame around messages.

ElementLooks likeMeans

Level 1

Three parties, four messages

The simplest useful sequence: a customer pays, the merchant asks the PSP to authorise, the answer comes back.

NewParticipants, synchronous calls, return messages.

Steps to draw it

  • 1. List participants left to right, starting with whoever triggers the flow.
  • 2. Draw each call as a solid arrow, in time order, top to bottom.
  • 3. Draw each reply as a dashed arrow back.
  • 4. Label calls as actions ("Authorise payment") and replies as results.

Why it matters

The vertical order is the whole point: it settles arguments about what happens before what. If two people disagree on the sequence, the diagram forces the answer.

Level 2

Bring in the next system

The PSP does not decide alone; it asks the customer's bank.

NewFourth participant (Issuer bank) and a nested call and reply.

Steps

  • 1. Add the issuer as a new participant on the right.
  • 2. Insert the PSP's call and the issuer's reply between the PSP's incoming call and its reply.
  • 3. Keep every call matched to a reply.

Why it matters

Nested calls show dependency: the merchant's response time includes the issuer's. That is where latency and timeout questions come from.

Pro note Add activation bars (thin boxes on a lifeline) in a tool when you need to show how long each participant is busy and waiting. They make blocking calls visible.
Level 3

Alternatives with alt fragments

Payments can be approved or declined, and the customer sees a different outcome in each case.

NewAn alt fragment with two guarded branches.

Steps

  • 1. Draw a frame around the messages that depend on the outcome.
  • 2. Label it alt and write the first condition in brackets: [approved].
  • 3. Split it with a dashed line and write the other condition: [declined].
  • 4. Cover every case so the conditions are exhaustive.

Why it matters

Alt is the sequence-diagram equivalent of a gateway. Each branch is a test case waiting to be written.

Pro note Use opt for a single optional branch with no else. Keep fragments shallow: more than two levels of nesting usually means you should split the diagram.
Level 4

Adding 3DS authentication

Strong customer authentication adds a challenge before authorisation, but only when it is required.

NewA second alt for the challenge, a self message for an exemption, and the customer interacting directly with the issuer.

Steps

  • 1. Place an alt before the authorisation request.
  • 2. In the first branch, redirect the customer, challenge them and return the result.
  • 3. In the other branch, show the frictionless path with a self message.
  • 4. Rejoin the main flow after the frame.

Why it matters

This is the realistic shape of a European card payment: two decision points, two sets of outcomes. Showing both stops requirements assuming every payment is a simple call.

Pro note Simplified on purpose. In a real flow, the exact participants (access control server, directory servers) and redirect mechanics depend on the PSP and the 3DS version.
Level 5

Asynchronous results and retries

The final status often arrives later as a webhook, and the PSP resends it until the merchant acknowledges.

NewIdempotency key on the call, asynchronous webhook, loop fragment, internal verification step.

Steps

  • 1. Label the original call with its idempotency key.
  • 2. Draw the webhook as an asynchronous message (solid, open arrowhead).
  • 3. Wrap the delivery and acknowledgement in a loop with its stop condition.
  • 4. Show the merchant verifying the signature and updating the order.

Why it matters

It exposes requirements nobody states aloud: duplicate webhooks must be harmless, and the order cannot rely on the synchronous answer alone.

Pro note Many PSPs retry webhook delivery with backoff until they get a success response, but timings and rules differ, so confirm the specifics in the provider's documentation.

For experienced practitioners

Sequence or BPMN, and common mistakes

BPMNSequence diagram
Question it answersWho does which step in the business process?In what order do the systems interact?
AudienceBusiness, operations, analystsDevelopers, architects, testers
StrengthDecisions, roles, exceptions, durationsAPI calls, timing, sync vs async, dependencies
WeaknessHides technical interaction detailGets unreadable with many branches

Conventions that age well

  • Name participants by role or system, not product version.
  • Label messages with the operation, and returns with the outcome.
  • Keep to five to seven participants.
  • Use ref to link detail rather than growing one diagram.
  • One scenario per diagram, with variants as fragments or separate pages.

Mistakes I see most

  • Treating it as a flowchart and modelling every branch.
  • Missing return messages, so waiting is invisible.
  • Showing the happy path only.
  • Mixing business steps and technical calls.
  • Ignoring asynchronous behaviour and retries.
Takeaways

What to take away

If you are new

  • Participants, messages and time downward are enough to start.
  • Add one concept at a time: a second system, then alternatives, then async.
  • Draw the failure path as soon as the happy path works.

If you already draw them

  • Use async arrows and loops to surface retry and idempotency requirements.
  • Pair with BPMN: process for the business, sequence for the integration.
  • Treat every alt branch as a test scenario and every message as an interface to specify.
