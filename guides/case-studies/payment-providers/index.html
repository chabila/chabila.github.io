Part 1 — Fundamentals

The provider landscape

"Payment provider" hides a lot of variety. Picking the wrong category for a given need is a more common mistake than picking the wrong specific vendor.

e.g. Adyen, Stripe

Full-stack acquirer + gateway

Combines acquiring, gateway, and risk/fraud tooling in one platform, often with issuing capabilities layered on top.

When it fits: reducing vendor count and simplifying reconciliation — one settlement file instead of several.

e.g. Klarna, Clearpay

BNPL / alternative financing providers

Pay-later or instalment methods where the provider takes on the consumer credit risk, not the merchant.

When it fits: lifts conversion and average order value, but brings its own regulatory treatment and different settlement timing — the provider often pays the merchant upfront and collects from the consumer over time.

e.g. PayPal, Apple Pay, Google Pay

Digital wallets

Tokenized, device- or account-based methods, usually layered on top of a PSP rather than replacing it.

When it fits: reduces checkout friction — especially on mobile — but needs its own eligibility and display requirements, separate from the card flow.

e.g. ACI Worldwide, legacy bank platforms

Enterprise / legacy platforms

Older, often bank-grade platforms with deep enterprise integration but more complex APIs — sometimes SOAP/XML rather than modern REST/JSON.

When it fits: common in large, established retail and financial environments; migrating off one is a project type in its own right.

e.g. iDEAL, Bancontact, Sofort, SEPA Direct Debit

Local / alternative payment methods (APMs)

Market-specific methods that are essential for conversion in particular countries, not optional extras.

When it fits: any genuine multi-market rollout — a Netherlands checkout without iDEAL loses conversion that no amount of UX polish recovers.

Visa, Mastercard, Amex

Card networks (not PSPs)

Set the rulebook — interchange, scheme fees, chargeback rules — that every PSP operates within, even though a BA rarely integrates with them directly.

When it matters: every card-payment requirement operates inside a rulebook you didn't write and can't negotiate around.

Payment orchestration platforms — a layer that sits above multiple PSPs and routes transactions dynamically — are covered in Part 5, since they're less a "provider type" and more a response to having outgrown a single-PSP setup.

APIs & integration patterns

The specific technical shapes a payment integration takes, and the decisions embedded in each one.

REST/JSON payment APIs

The modern default

Most current-generation PSPs (Adyen's Checkout API, Stripe's Payment Intents API) expose REST/JSON — synchronous request/response for initiating a payment, with async events layered on top.

Hosted vs. embedded checkout

A PCI-scope decision, not just a UX one

Redirecting to a PSP-hosted page keeps card data off your servers entirely (SAQ A). Embedding a client-side component (Drop-in, Elements) still keeps raw card data out of your backend, but pulls you into a heavier compliance tier (SAQ A-EP) than a full redirect — a requirement decision with real compliance cost attached.

Webhooks / async notifications

Authorisation isn't always synchronous

Capture confirmations, chargebacks, and refund events often arrive as asynchronous webhooks, sometimes minutes or hours later. Any requirement that assumes an instant, synchronous confirmation will eventually be wrong.

3DS2 / SCA authentication flow

Challenge vs. frictionless, and the exemption logic between them

PSD2's Strong Customer Authentication requirement means every flow needs to account for both a challenge (OTP/biometric step) and frictionless outcomes, plus the exemption rules (low-value, trusted beneficiary) that avoid unnecessary friction when they apply.

Tokenization APIs

Storing a credential, not a card number

Card-on-file for repeat purchases or subscriptions should always go through a tokenization API — the token is reusable, the raw PAN never needs to touch your systems again.

Payouts / disbursement APIs

For splitting funds across multiple parties

Marketplace and platform models need a payouts API to split a single payment across multiple sellers or partners — a materially different integration from a simple merchant-of-record flow.

Reconciliation / reporting APIs

Where finance actually gets their data

Settlement files and transaction reports, usually pulled via a separate reporting API or SFTP drop — this is what finance and treasury are actually consuming, distinct from the transactional API entirely.

Part 2 — Where it goes wrong

Precautions for payment-related decisions

Ten things worth checking before a payment requirement ships — most learned the expensive way, on someone's project, if not mine.

01 · PCI-DSS SCOPE

Every architecture choice changes your compliance scope

Hosted vs. embedded vs. direct API each carry a different SAQ tier. Involve security/compliance before the architecture is locked in, not after.

02 · IDEMPOTENCY

Retries must never double-charge

Any requirement involving payment creation needs an explicit idempotency-key strategy — network retries are a certainty, not an edge case.

03 · CURRENCY PRECISION

Not every currency has decimal places

Zero-decimal currencies (JPY, for example) break naive amount-handling logic silently — a rounding assumption that works for GBP/EUR can quietly corrupt reconciliation elsewhere.

04 · SCA EXEMPTIONS

Missing exemption logic adds friction you don't need

Without explicit low-value or trusted-beneficiary exemption handling, every transaction defaults to the friction-heavy path — a conversion cost nobody notices until someone checks the numbers.

05 · DATA MINIMISATION

Tokenize early, store nothing you don't need

The safest card data is the card data you never touch. Push tokenization as early in the flow as the PSP allows.

06 · PSP REDUNDANCY

A single PSP is a single point of failure

When a primary PSP has an outage, can traffic fail over to a backup? That's an architecture decision made at design time, not something you can retrofit during an incident.

07 · CHARGEBACK EVIDENCE

Disputes are resolved on evidence captured at the time

A chargeback is contested months later using whatever evidence was captured at transaction time — device data, delivery confirmation, IP address. Capture it then, because you can't go back and get it later.

08 · VENDOR LOCK-IN

Tokens don't always travel between PSPs

A proprietary token format can make switching PSPs later far more expensive than the original integration — a decision with a multi-year tail most requirements don't account for.

09 · REGULATORY DIVERGENCE

PSD2 isn't a global standard

A rollout spanning EU/UK and the US needs a market-by-market compliance review — a single global requirement spec will be quietly wrong somewhere.

10 · SANDBOX PARITY

Sandbox behaviour doesn't always match production

Certain decline codes, edge-case timing, and rare failure modes only show up in production. Treat a clean sandbox certification as necessary, not sufficient.

Part 3 — Who else needs to be involved

Teams involved

A payment decision rarely belongs to one team. This is who else is usually affected, and what they specifically own.

Payments / Product

Owns the PSP relationship and roadmap — which methods to support, which markets to prioritise, contract renegotiation timing.

Engineering / Integration

Builds and maintains the actual API integration, webhook handling, and retry/idempotency logic.

Security / InfoSec

Owns PCI-DSS scope assessment and sign-off, penetration testing, and data-handling review before anything touching card data ships.

Compliance / Legal

Reviews regulatory obligations (PSD2, local payment regulation), and negotiates the data processing terms in the PSP contract itself.

Finance / Treasury

Owns settlement reconciliation, FX exposure on multi-currency flows, and the fee structure — including renegotiating it as volume grows.

Fraud / Risk

Configures fraud rules and thresholds, monitors chargeback rate against network limits that can trigger penalties if breached.

Customer Support / Ops

Handles payment-related customer queries and processes refunds — needs visibility into transaction status without escalating every query to engineering.

QA / Test

Tests the full scenario set including decline paths, 3DS challenge flows, and webhook timing — not just the happy path.

Architecture

Owns the longer-term integration pattern decisions — especially multi-PSP routing, orchestration, and how portable the setup stays over time.

PSP implementation team External

The provider's own account and implementation managers — an external stakeholder, but one whose relationship management matters as much as any internal team's.

Part 4 — What you didn't ask for, but should know

A few things worth flagging beyond the brief

Not explicitly requested, but close enough to this topic that leaving them out would make the reference incomplete.

Payment orchestration platforms

A layer that sits above multiple PSPs and routes transactions dynamically — by cost, success rate, or redundancy. Relevant once a business has outgrown a single-PSP setup and needs smart routing or failover without a full re-integration each time.

Recurring billing & card lifecycle management

Subscription and recurring payments need account-updater services — most PSPs offer one — to automatically refresh expired or reissued card details, otherwise involuntary churn from failed renewals becomes a silent revenue leak.

Open Banking / Pay by Bank

An emerging account-to-account payment category that bypasses card networks entirely, typically at lower cost. Still early relative to card and wallet volume, but growing fast enough that a payments roadmap without it is already slightly behind.

BNPL's shifting regulatory status

Buy-now-pay-later has moved from largely unregulated to increasingly regulated (the FCA bringing BNPL into its remit in the UK) — a requirement written a year ago may already need revisiting against updated affordability-check and disclosure obligations.

Underneath all of it

Principles that outlast any specific provider

01

The PSP relationship is a partnership, not a purchase

Rate renegotiation, roadmap alignment, and account management are ongoing work — a PSP integration doesn't end at go-live, it starts an ongoing relationship.

02

Every payment requirement has three readers

Engineering, compliance, and finance will all read the same requirement looking for different things. Write it so all three get what they need without asking.

03

Test the failure paths harder than the happy path

Declines, timeouts, partial webhooks, and 3DS challenges are where payment integrations actually break in production — the happy path rarely surprises anyone.

04

A payment decision outlives the person who made it

PSP contracts run for years. Document the rationale behind a routing or provider choice — the next BA inheriting it deserves to know why, not just what.
