Production issues in retail: what the BA is actually doing

When a checkout breaks at 2am on a Tuesday, the response looks nothing like when it breaks at 2am on Black Friday — same fault, completely different job. This walks through six production scenarios a retail BA actually faces, what the role looks like in detail in each one, and exactly how peak season changes the calculus.

Chabila Borkar· Senior Business Analyst, Payments & eCommerce
A quick note on how to read this

Engineering fixes the code. The BA's job during an incident is different and just as critical: translating a technical fault into a business decision someone can actually act on, fast enough that the decision doesn't become the bottleneck. This is what that looks like across the incidents that come up again and again in retail, and why the exact same fault demands a completely different response depending on when it happens.

Part 1 — Fundamentals

Why this is a BA's job, not just engineering's

A production incident generates two problems at once: a technical one and a business one. Engineering owns the first. Someone has to own the second — translating severity into impact, impact into priority, and priority into a decision someone with authority can actually make under pressure. That's the BA's seat at the table, and it's every bit as time-critical as the fix itself.

Peak vs. non-peak: the fundamental shift

This is the distinction that changes everything else in this guide. The same incident, the same root cause, gets an entirely different BA response depending on which side of this line it falls on.

Non-peak
Peak (Black Friday / Cyber Monday)
Response expectation
Hours are often acceptable for lower-severity issues, following the standard severity matrix.
Minutes. Every minute of degraded checkout is quantifiable, real-time lost revenue.
Escalation structure
Normal on-call rotation, standard severity-based paging.
Dedicated war room, all-hands bridge, executive visibility from minute one.
Communication cadence
As-needed updates; a status page update if customer-facing.
Continuous, tight cadence — often every 15 minutes — to a pre-agreed stakeholder list.
Root cause vs. fix
Full root-cause analysis is usually prioritised before a permanent fix ships.
Fastest safe mitigation first; full RCA explicitly deferred until after peak, with the resulting debt documented, not forgotten.
Change freeze
None — normal deployment cadence continues.
Typically already in effect — any fix has to respect the freeze scope or go through an emergency exception process.
Fix philosophy
Prefer the correct long-term fix — there's time to do it properly.

Decision authority

Pre-agreed emergency authority, established before peak began — not decided live, under pressure, for the first time.
The single biggest predictor of how well a peak incident goes isn't how the war room performs on the day — it's whether the failover runbooks, decision authority, and change-freeze exceptions were defined weeks earlier, when nobody was under pressure to get them right.
Part 2 — Scenario by scenario

Six production issues, and the BA's role in detail

Each of these follows the same shape: what's actually happening, what the BA is doing in detail while it's happening, and how peak season changes that response specifically for this scenario.

Scenario 01

Checkout & payment failures

A PSP timeout spike, an elevated decline rate, or a checkout step throwing errors — customers reach the final step and can't complete a purchase.

The BA's role in detail

First job is establishing whether the fault sits with the PSP or with the merchant's own integration, pulling transaction logs and the PSP's status page in parallel with engineering rather than waiting for their verdict. The real value-add is translating a raw number — "12% decline rate" — into a business statement everyone in the room can act on: roughly how much revenue is being lost per hour if this continues. That's what actually gets a failover decision moving. If failing over to a secondary PSP is on the table, the BA defines exactly what "working" means for that failover — which payment methods, which markets — so engineering and QA aren't verifying against a guess. Customer-facing messaging (a banner, pausing a specific payment method proactively) is also a BA-owned call, made jointly with support and comms, since it's a business risk decision, not a technical one.

Non-peakUsually a single-PSP issue handled through standard vendor escalation — pause the affected method for a few hours, wait for their fix, no drama.
PeakThe failover decision has to happen in minutes with revenue bleeding by the second. If a failover runbook wasn't built and tested before peak, the BA is now improvising a routing decision live — exactly the situation pre-peak planning exists to prevent.
Scenario 02

Site performance & availability

Page load times degrade under load, or specific journeys start timing out — sometimes short of a full outage, which makes it harder to triage than a clean "the site is down."

The BA's role in detail

Translates raw technical severity (response time, error rate) into which customer journeys are actually broken — checkout still working while browse is slow is a completely different priority than the reverse, and that distinction usually isn't obvious from a dashboard alone. Works with ops to weigh a stopgap like disabling a resource-heavy feature (personalised recommendations, for instance) against letting the degradation continue, a trade-off that's a genuine business call, not purely a technical one. Keeps a real-time incident timeline throughout, since that becomes the backbone of both the eventual RCA and any customer-facing SLA conversation afterward.

Non-peakA slow page is a bug ticket, scheduled into the next sprint like any other defect.
PeakThe same slowness at ten times the traffic is existential. The BA's role shifts to actively deciding which non-essential features to sacrifice to protect the checkout path — a call almost never made outside of peak.
Scenario 03

Inventory & stock sync issues

The OMS and the website disagree on stock levels — overselling (the site says available when it isn't) or the reverse (marked out of stock while units actually sit in the warehouse).

The BA's role in detail

Establishes the blast radius first — how many SKUs, how many orders already placed against stock that doesn't exist — by querying order and inventory data directly rather than waiting for a summary. Works with fulfilment to decide the remediation path for affected orders already placed: cancel and refund, source from another location, or delay with proactive communication. Drafts that customer communication itself, because "your item is out of stock" needs a very different tone and offer depending on whether it's a low-value item or something the customer specifically waited for.

Non-peakOverselling typically affects a handful of orders — resolved with a manual apology and refund, low drama.
PeakThe same fault can affect thousands of orders within minutes given transaction volume. Priority shifts immediately to pulling the SKU from sale to stop the bleeding — individual order remediation waits until the immediate cause is actually closed.
Scenario 04

Pricing & promotion errors

A promo code applies the wrong discount, prices display incorrectly, or discounts stack in a way nobody intended.

The BA's role in detail

Has to surface the uncomfortable commercial question fast: honour the incorrect price already applied to live orders, or cancel them? That's a brand and legal risk decision as much as a technical one, and the BA is usually the one framing that trade-off clearly enough for whoever holds the authority to actually decide — often legal or commercial leadership, brought in immediately rather than after the fact. Equally important is confirming actual scope quickly: one promo code, or the whole pricing engine — because the response differs enormously and guessing wrong wastes the most valuable early minutes.

Non-peakA pricing bug affecting a few dozen orders is usually just honoured as goodwill — low commercial risk, low ceremony.
PeakThe same bug at Black Friday volume can represent material financial exposure within minutes, meaning the honour-or-cancel decision needs to escalate to much more senior stakeholders, much faster, than it would on an ordinary Tuesday.
Scenario 05

Carrier & fulfilment integration failures

A carrier's tracking API goes down, or the fulfilment integration stops syncing order status between systems.

The BA's role in detail

Assesses immediately whether this is customer-visible (tracking info simply not updating) or operational (orders genuinely stalled in fulfilment) — these carry completely different urgency and get conflated far too often in the first confused minutes of an incident. If the automated sync is down for an extended period, coordinates a manual workaround with ops and the warehouse team, and defines precisely what data needs reconciling once the integration is restored, so nothing about order state gets silently lost in the gap.

Non-peakA few hours of carrier downtime is an inconvenience, resolved with a delayed batch sync once service resumes — no lasting impact.
PeakThe same downtime layered on peak volume risks a fulfilment backlog that can take days to clear even after the root cause is fixed — the BA has to push for proactive delivery-delay communication well before customers notice anything themselves.
Scenario 06

Fraud spikes & security incidents

A card-testing attack (automated small transactions validating stolen card numbers) or a suspected exposure of customer data.

The BA's role in detail

Works closely with the fraud/risk team to establish what's actually happening — a card-testing spike can look almost identical to legitimate demand at a glance, and misreading which one it is wastes critical time in either direction. If the incident touches customer data, the role shifts heavily toward coordinating with legal and compliance on notification obligations and timelines, which are often the single highest-stakes deadlines anywhere in the incident, regulatory rather than technical.

Non-peakA fraud spike is usually isolated and manageable through normal fraud-rule tuning, without much drama.
PeakA card-testing attack during Black Friday can be genuinely hard to distinguish from legitimate demand in real time — and being too aggressive with fraud rules risks blocking real customers during the highest-revenue hours of the year, a tension that barely exists the rest of the year.
Part 3

Principles that hold across every scenario

01

The BA's job in an incident is translation, not diagnosis

Engineering diagnoses the fault. The BA translates its severity into business impact and turns that into a decision someone can actually make — a different skill, equally time-critical.

02

Peak readiness is built before peak, not during it

Failover runbooks, pre-agreed decision authority, and war-room structure all need to exist before Black Friday. Inventing them live is what turns a bad incident into a very bad one.

03

A quick fix during peak is a decision, not a shortcut

Document the technical debt explicitly the moment it's created, or it quietly becomes permanent the week after peak ends and everyone moves on to the next priority.

04

The post-incident write-up is where the next incident gets shorter

RCA discipline pays off precisely when you're too busy to want to do it — skip it once and the same root cause resurfaces at the worst possible time next peak.
