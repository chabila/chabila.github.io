Stakeholder management: who, and how, not just a RACI chart

Most stakeholder management advice stops at "build a RACI." The actual skill is knowing that a legal reviewer, a developer, and a customer need three completely different conversations about the same decision — and that the right approach for a new feature request is close to wrong for a live incident.

Chabila Borkar· Senior Business Analyst, Payments & eCommerce
A quick note on how to read this

I've run this from both ends — in a room with an engineering team at 9am and a client steering committee at 2pm, on the same requirement. What follows is the framework I actually use, not the idealised version from a BA certification course. It starts with definitions and frameworks, then gets specific about roles and scenarios, because that's where most stakeholder advice stops being useful.

Part 1 — Fundamentals

What is a stakeholder?

A stakeholder is anyone who can affect, or is affected by, the initiative — not just the people invited to the meeting. That distinction matters more than it sounds.

The core definition

Affects, or is affected by

A stakeholder has an interest in the outcome or the influence to shape it — sometimes both, sometimes neither obviously, which is exactly why mapping matters more than gut feel.

Stakeholder vs. sponsor

Not every stakeholder funds the work

A sponsor holds budget and ultimate accountability. A stakeholder might have neither, and still be essential — a compliance officer with veto power over a specific clause isn't funding anything.

Stakeholder vs. user

Not every stakeholder uses the thing

An end customer is a stakeholder because they're affected by the outcome. A finance controller reviewing revenue impact is a stakeholder without ever touching the product.

The common mistake

Conflating "loud" with "important"

The stakeholder who emails the most isn't automatically the one whose sign-off actually matters. Confusing volume with influence is the single most common way stakeholder management goes wrong.

Classifying & mapping

Three frameworks I actually use, each doing a different job — none of them replaces judgement, but together they stop you relying on gut feel alone.

Power / Interest Grid
(Mendelow's Matrix)

The starting point for any new initiative

Plots stakeholders on two axes — how much influence they have, and how much they care about this specific outcome. Four quadrants fall out of it, shown below.

RACI

Per decision, not per person

Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed — the mistake most teams make is building one RACI for the whole project. Build it per deliverable or decision; the same person is often Accountable on one decision and merely Informed on the next.

Salience Model
(power, legitimacy, urgency)

For stakeholders whose influence is situational

Power/interest alone misses stakeholders like legal or compliance, who have low day-to-day interest but become highly salient — and non-negotiable — the moment a specific regulatory question arises. Salience captures that swing.

Stakeholder register

The living document underneath all three

Name, role, interest, influence, preferred communication channel, engagement strategy, escalation path — reviewed at every phase, because stakeholders move between quadrants as the project moves through its lifecycle.

The power/interest grid in practice

High power · High interest

Manage closely

Your sponsor, your lead architect on a major redesign. Engage frequently, involve in real decisions, never surprise them.

High power · Low interest

Keep satisfied

A senior exec who cares about the outcome but not the detail. Short, infrequent, high-level updates — don't bury them in detail they didn't ask for.

Low power · High interest

Keep informed

A support team who'll live with the change daily but can't block it. Regular updates build goodwill and surface real operational risk early.

Low power · Low interest

Monitor

Minimal effort — but check periodically, because low interest today doesn't mean low interest once the change actually lands on their desk.

The grid is a snapshot, not a verdict. The single biggest failure mode in stakeholder mapping is treating the quadrant as permanent — someone in "Monitor" during discovery can land in "Manage Closely" by go-live, and the register needs to catch that shift before it catches you.

Who you're actually managing

Eleven roles that show up on almost every delivery I've worked, and what actually works with each — not the generic "communicate clearly" advice that applies to everyone and helps no one.

Customer / end user
Cares aboutWhether it solves their actual problem, with minimal friction.
Engage viaUser research, journey mapping, plain language — never internal jargon.
Common frictionRarely in the room; the requirement is a stand-in for a conversation that didn't happen.
Business / sponsor
Cares aboutROI, timeline, risk to revenue.
Engage viaBusiness case language, KPI framing, executive summary before detail.
Common frictionWants certainty before scope is fully known — manage that expectation early, not at the review.
IT / operations
Cares aboutSupportability and operational load after go-live.
Engage viaRunbooks, operational readiness reviews, explicit ownership boundaries.
Common frictionBrought in too late, inheriting decisions they never had a chance to shape.
Technical / developers
Cares aboutUnambiguous, testable requirements — not a solution disguised as one.
Engage viaClear acceptance criteria, and direct access to ask "why", not just "what".
Common frictionVague requirements create rework; devs need the rationale to judge edge cases the spec missed.
Architects
Cares aboutLong-term maintainability and consistency with platform standards.
Engage viaArchitecture Decision Records, involvement before solutioning is locked in.
Common frictionLooped in after a solution is already assumed, forcing a rework of the underlying design.
QA / test
Cares aboutTestability, edge cases, and enough lead time to plan coverage.
Engage viaInvolve at the acceptance-criteria stage, not just when test cases are due.
Common frictionAcceptance criteria written without QA input often aren't actually testable as written.
Product Owner
Cares aboutBacklog priority, value delivered per sprint, protecting scope.
Engage viaTransparent trade-off conversations grounded in priority rationale.
Common frictionOverlap with the BA role is ambiguous on some teams — agree early who owns "what" versus "how it's specified".
Legal
Cares aboutContractual exposure, liability, regulatory wording accuracy.
Engage viaBring in early on anything customer-facing or contractual — share actual proposed wording, not a summary.
Common frictionTreated as a rubber-stamp at the end instead of an early input, causing late-stage rewrites.
Compliance
Cares aboutRegulatory obligations and a clean audit trail.
Engage viaDocumented decision rationale, early flagging of anything touching a regulated process.
Common frictionConcerns surface after build has started, because they weren't looped in during requirements.
Payments team
Cares aboutSettlement accuracy, transaction success rates, PSP relationship terms.
Engage viaReconciliation data and a clear before/after view of transaction flow impact.
Common friction"Front-end only" changes often carry routing or reconciliation implications this team needs to weigh in on.
Finance
Cares aboutRevenue recognition impact, budget tracking, accurate reporting.
Engage viaExplicit articulation of financial impact — e.g., how a discount logic change shifts recognition timing.
Common frictionOnly consulted once something breaks their reporting, rather than proactively at design time.
Part 2 — By scenario across the lifecycle

New requirements

The widest net you'll cast in the whole lifecycle — and the stage where under-consulting costs the most later.

Discovery

Widen before you narrow

Who's central
Business / customer Architects Product Owner Legal / Compliance (if regulated)
How to approach it

Talk to more stakeholders than you think you need before converging. Ask "why" before "what" — the business/customer conversation should shape the requirement, not just confirm one you'd already assumed. Sequence matters: understand the need first, sanity-check feasibility with architects and technical leads second, prioritise with the PO third, and loop in legal/compliance early if it touches anything regulated.

Watch for: jumping straight to developers with a half-formed idea, skipping the business rationale that would have shaped a better requirement in the first place.
Part 2 — continued

Existing / BAU changes

Routine enhancement requests to something already live — where the skill is knowing how little ceremony is actually needed.

Who's central

The team already accountable for that area

Usually a narrower set than a new initiative — the dev/QA pairing who owns that part of the system, plus the business stakeholder who can confirm the value is still real.

How to approach it

Lighter touch, reused context

Check the existing RAID log and decision history before re-discovering from scratch. Confirm with the accountable business stakeholder, then route directly to dev/QA — no need to reassemble the full discovery cast for a small, well-understood change.

Watch for

Ceremony creep

Treating every small change with the same process as a new initiative slows delivery and quietly irritates stakeholders who've seen this exact pattern before — proportionality is part of managing them well.

Part 2 — continued

Mid-scope changes

Scope shifting while delivery is already underway — the scenario where transparency matters more than speed.

In flight

Show the cost before you agree to it

Who's central
Product Owner Architects / developers Original requesting stakeholder
How to approach it

Capture the change formally as a change request rather than absorbing it quietly. Assess impact with the technical team first, then present the trade-off — time, rework, risk — to the business/PO before proceeding. Get explicit sign-off, not silent acceptance. Check the change still serves the original requester's actual need, not just the new idea that arrived mid-delivery.

Watch for: absorbing scope quietly to avoid a difficult conversation — it erodes your ability to say no next time, and the cost doesn't disappear, it just becomes invisible until it isn't.
Part 2 — continued

Live site issues

The scenario that looks nothing like the other three — speed and calm replace thoroughness, and the BA's job is as much about managing communication as understanding the fix.

Who's central

Everyone, in parallel, not in sequence

IT/ops for diagnosis, technical/developers for the fix, business for customer-impact assessment, legal/compliance if there's a data or regulatory angle, customer-facing teams for comms — all moving at once, not queued one after another.

How to approach it

Short, factual, on a cadence

Separate "what we know" from "what we're investigating" in every update. A regular short status beats an occasional comprehensive one — stakeholders in an incident want to know you're on it, not read a full root-cause analysis mid-fire. Save the complete RCA and formal sign-off for after resolution.

Watch for

Disappearing into the fix

It's tempting to go heads-down with the technical team and let communication lapse. Managing the stakeholder update cadence is equally the job during an incident — not a distraction from the real work.

Underneath all four scenarios

Principles that hold regardless of scenario

01

The register is alive, not a one-time exercise

Revisit it at every phase. Power and interest shift constantly — someone irrelevant at discovery can be the single blocker at go-live.

02

Loud isn't the same as important

The frameworks in Part 1 exist precisely to catch this. Don't let the most vocal stakeholder set your priority by volume alone.

03

Match the artefact to the audience

A business case for the sponsor, an ADR for the architect, a runbook for ops — the same decision, three completely different documents, each earning its keep for a different reader.

04

Silence is not agreement

Especially true of legal and compliance. Get explicit sign-off, not the absence of an objection — the difference matters enormously the first time something goes wrong.

If you've got a stakeholder scenario that doesn't fit neatly into these four — that's usually the interesting one. Happy to compare notes.
